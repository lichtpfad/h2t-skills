# Search Technique Toolkit

Use this when a topic search feels shallow or one-sided — techniques for widening a search,
following a citation trail, checking you actually covered the topic, and testing a specific
hypothesis rather than just "exploring."

## Search expansion techniques

Apply at least two of these when a search on a topic isn't turning up enough, or keeps
returning the same few obvious results:

| Technique | What to do |
|---|---|
| Cross-domain analogy | Look for an analogous mechanism or pattern in an unrelated field — it sometimes surfaces vocabulary or evidence your direct search misses |
| Assumption reversal | Ask "what if the conventional view is wrong?" and search specifically for evidence of that |
| Scale shifting | Look at the phenomenon at a different level (e.g., molecular vs. cellular vs. systemic vs. population; or unit-level vs. market-level) |
| Constraint removal | Ask "what if the key limitation didn't exist?" — search for how the picture changes without it |
| Temporal shifting | Ask how understanding of this has changed over time — what was once accepted but later disproven? |

## Citation chaining

After finding a key source, expand outward from it — keyword search alone misses work that
only cites or is cited by that source:

- **Forward citations** — who cites this source? (newer work building on or challenging it)
- **Backward citations** — what does this source itself cite? (the foundational work behind it)

## Coverage and diversity check

Before treating a topic as "covered," check for gaps that keyword search tends to leave
behind — searches naturally gravitate toward what's novel or controversial and skip the
unglamorous but important parts of a topic:

1. Enumerate the full taxonomy of the domain being researched (see
   `domain-query-frameworks.md` for a starting checklist per domain).
2. Check that every category in that taxonomy has at least one search or source behind it.
3. Explicitly flag "boring but important" items that might have been skipped because they
   aren't novel or controversial (they're often high-evidence and simply unexciting).
4. If a category has nothing behind it, either fold it into an existing search or run an
   additional one — don't leave it silently uncovered.

## Hypothesis-testing search pattern

When you're not just exploring a topic but testing a specific claim or hypothesis, search in
both directions on purpose:

1. **Search for supporting evidence** — what backs the mechanism or prediction you're testing?
2. **Search for opposing evidence, deliberately** — null results, failed replications,
   alternative explanations for the same data. Ask: "if this hypothesis were wrong, what would
   the data look like?" and go look for that.
3. **Look for distinguishing evidence** — what result would differentiate this hypothesis from
   a competing one, rather than being consistent with both?
4. **Check boundary conditions** — under what conditions does the hypothesis stop holding?
5. **Test the hypothesis's own hidden assumptions** — a hypothesis usually smuggles in
   assumptions of its own (e.g., that a mechanism observed in one context transfers
   unchanged to another). List them and check each one separately, rather than only
   checking the hypothesis's headline prediction.

## Record what you searched

Keep a short, plain record alongside any nontrivial search: which queries you ran, which
sources/databases you searched, and roughly how many results each returned. This is what
lets you (or anyone else) tell later whether a gap in the findings is a real gap in the
evidence or just a gap in the search — and it's what the coverage check above is verifying
against.
